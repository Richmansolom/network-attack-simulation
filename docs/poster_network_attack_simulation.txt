--- Abstract ---
Testing the Limits of Mathematical Models for Intrusion Detection

Mathematical models of network intrusion detection typically assume idealized conditions: independent detection events, stationary Poisson attack arrivals, and predictable exponential performance degradation. This project develops a discrete-event simulation framework to systematically test where these assumptions hold and where they break down.

The simulation models three core processes: (1) binomial detection, where each malicious packet is independently detected with probability p; (2) Poisson attack arrivals with rate λ and exponentially distributed inter-arrival times; and (3) throughput degradation following T(t) = T₀ e^(−αt). The modular architecture includes validated components for attack generation, network buffering, IDS behavior, and metrics collection.

Validation testing confirms that the simulation accurately reproduces theoretical distributions. Simulated attack counts match Poisson expectations, inter-arrival times pass Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests for exponentiality, and detection rates fall within 95% confidence intervals of binomial predictions.

With this validated platform, we conduct systematic experiments across parameter ranges: detection probability (p = 0.3–0.95 in the H1-focused analysis layer), attack intensity (λ = 0.1–2.0 per minute), and decay rate (α = 0.1–1.0). We hypothesize that model accuracy will degrade under high attack intensity and low detection probability, where independence assumptions are most likely violated.

This framework enables reproducible cybersecurity research, allowing researchers to stress-test theoretical assumptions before real-world deployment.

--- Introduction ---
Real networks mix correlation, saturation, and resource contention; textbook formulas assume cleaner conditions. We implement a Phase 2 discrete-event simulator (event clock, Poisson attack generator, IDS, network with throughput decay, metrics) and a Phase 3 pipeline (full factorial design, 30 replications per condition, five hypotheses H1–H5, effect sizes, Bonferroni correction, figures). The goal is to show where classical stochastic models align with simulation and where finite data, saturation, or load stress the assumptions—especially at low p and high λ.

--- Objective ---
(1) Deliver a reproducible codebase whose outputs match the Phase 3 data dictionary. (2) Run the standard factorial (5 IDS levels from p = 0.30–0.95 × 4 attack rates × 3 decay rates × 4 packet counts). (3) Test H1–H5 with appropriate statistics and interpret practical vs. statistical significance. (4) Report limitations (e.g., degenerate tests when detection saturates near 1).

--- Hypothesis — Block 1 (H1) ---
H1 (Binomial detection at the attack level). For n independent packet inspections per attack with per-packet detection probability p, the probability of detecting at least one malicious packet is P = 1 − (1 − p)^n. We compare simulated attack-level detection rates to this value for each (p, n). The main factorial uses five fixed levels of p (0.30, 0.50, 0.70, 0.85, 0.95) crossed with all other factors (same 240-condition design as H2–H5). Where replications are degenerate (zero variance), we use an exact binomial test on pooled successes and trials.

--- Hypothesis — Block 2 (H2 & H3) ---
H2 (Poisson attack counts). Over a fixed duration, total attacks follow a Poisson distribution with mean λ × duration (consistent units). We compare observed counts to Poisson expectations (e.g., chi-square on interval counts with expected frequencies scaled to match observed totals).

H3 (Exponential inter-arrival times). For a Poisson attack process, inter-arrival times are exponential with mean 1/λ. We use Kolmogorov–Smirnov and mean checks on inter-arrival samples stored from the generator to reduce finite-window bias.

--- Hypothesis — Block 3 (H4 & H5) ---
H4 (Throughput decay). Throughput decays as T(t) = T₀ e^(−αt). We fit the decay to the simulated throughput time series and compare to the configured α (e.g., R², bias).

H5 (Cross-factor behavior). We summarize detection rates and prediction error across combinations of p and λ (heatmaps, interaction plots; optional ANOVA-style summary). Runs with zero attacks are excluded where detection error would be misleading.

--- Methods (top) — Simulation design ---
Standard factorial for the main study: 5 × 4 × 3 × 4 = 240 conditions (p ∈ {0.30, 0.50, 0.70, 0.85, 0.95}; λ ∈ {0.2,0.5,1.0,2.0} min⁻¹; α ∈ {0.1,0.3,0.5}; packets per attack ∈ {10,25,50,100}). Thirty replications per condition → 7,200 runs in the primary results file. Random seeds: base_seed + run_id.

--- Methods (middle) — Statistical analysis & quality checks ---
H1: one-sample t-test vs theoretical P; exact binomial on pooled data when variance is zero. H2: chi-square. H3: K–S. H4: nonlinear fit. H5: grouped summaries and interaction visualization. Bonferroni α/5 across hypotheses; Cohen’s d for H1; 95% CIs on plots. Quality checks before long batches: detection sanity at high (p,n); expected attack count vs λ×duration; throughput between 0 and T₀; variability across replications; CSV columns and JSON fields valid.

--- Summary ---
The simulator aligns with Poisson attack counts, exponential inter-arrivals (with known finite-sample caveats), and binomial-based attack-level detection where theory is not saturated. Throughput follows exponential decay when the network module implements T(t)=T₀e^(−αt). Under low p or low attack counts per run, sampling noise is large; under high (p,n), theory and simulation both approach detection probability 1. The factorial covers p from 0.30 to 0.95 for H1 as stated in the abstract. The framework supports reproducible comparison of idealized IDS and queueing assumptions to controlled simulation before deployment.

--- Future studies ---
Increase replications where effects are practically important but noisy. Richer attack models (bursts, correlated arrivals), IDS models beyond independent Bernoulli packets, multi-hop topology and queueing, trace-driven calibration. Power analysis for each hypothesis at targeted effect sizes.
